The Eight-Step Decision Process

1Identify the important facts.
2Identify the ethical issue(s) and the values in tension.
3Identify the stakeholders, obvious and non-obvious.
4Identify potential solutions, including creative combinations.
5Assess the solutions using the five ethical perspectives.
6Assess any new solutions that emerged from step 5.
7Act in accordance with your ethical analysis.
8Assess your actions and the outcomes. What did you learn?

Framework Limitations

No framework leads to the right answer on its own. Each has known failure modes; understanding them makes you a more careful thinker.

Rules can conflict, and most people allow exceptions when principles are stated broadly. The challenge is bounding a principle tightly enough that it remains truly universal, and balancing it against competing rights or duties when they point in different directions.
You cannot predict all outcomes, and measuring all costs and benefits is genuinely hard, especially for things like dignity, relationships, or future trust. A majority outcome can also disregard the rights of a minority. The book notes: "The ends don't justify the means" is a direct challenge to pure consequentialism.
This framework relies on individual feelings and identity. Some people with undeveloped ethical instincts can pass their own mirror test and sleep soundly having done terrible things. The disclosure test isn't foolproof either; public values can and do support unethical behavior.
Difficult to balance conflicting duties; people often lack agreement on which duties take priority. Legal duties are a floor, not a ceiling; many legal actions are still ethically questionable. Moral duties are the hardest to define and the easiest to rationalize away.
The danger of favoritism and nepotism is real. It is also easy to give greater weight to people who are similar to us (what the book calls psychological distance), which can lead to inadvertent discrimination. Ask: is my primary obligation to maintain this person's lifestyle, or to be a person of integrity for them?

Questions to Ask Yourself Every Time

  • Am I protecting others' rights and dignity?
  • Would I be willing to be the other party in this transaction?
  • Who benefits and who is harmed by my decision?
  • What kind of person would this action make me?
  • How would I feel if my reasoning appeared on the news?
  • Am I fulfilling my obligations to those closest to me and most vulnerable?
